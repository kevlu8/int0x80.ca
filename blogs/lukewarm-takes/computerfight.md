# My strict parents and my old computer inadvertently taught me a lot
### 2026-08-18

From the age of 8, I was fascinated by computers. I played around with VBS and made fork bombs on an old Windows XP laptop the size of my torso. I watched videos on YouTube about malware, and took apart old computers in our basement to see how they worked.

## Parents

Like any other kid, I also loved Roblox. But my parents were pretty strict about my screen time. I was allowed to play for one hour every week - Saturday from 7:00 PM to 8:00 PM - on a Dell Optiplex 780 computer that was probably older than I was. It had a Core 2 Duo E8600, 4GB of RAM, an ATI Radeon HD 3450, a 1TB hard disk drive, and a CPU fan that sounded like a helicopter. Not impressive by any means, but it was the computer I grew up with, and I loved it.

Anyway, when the COVID-19 pandemic hit (called 2019-nCoV back then), 12-year-old me was stuck at home with hours upon hours of time. Obviously, my parents didn't let me spend all that time playing games, so they tried various methods to prevent me from accessing the internet. One such thing they tried was to block my computer's local IP address on the router. But I figured out that I could just make Windows use a new IP address every time I connected to the router, so I would just turn Wi-fi off and back on again and be able to play games once more. This same trick also worked on my phone, which was my mom's old iPhone SE. I would enter the settings app and manually change my IP address to a new one and regain internet access.

And thus, our "Cold War" began. My parents would try various methods to block me from playing games, and I would find ways to bypass them. They tried uninstalling Roblox, but I would just move the files to a hidden directory only accessible by directly typing the path into the file explorer. They tried blocking Roblox's servers on the router, but I would just use a VPN to bypass it.

Eventually, they just took away my Wi-fi card (the motherboard didn't have one built in). And that was that.

## Old Computer

In some sense, I am pretty grateful for having had to use such an old computer. It was slow and horribly frustrating to use, and that was unacceptable to me. I wanted to make it faster - but how?

I tried convincing my parents to let me re-install the OS (because it was running 32-bit Windows 10 on a perfectly 64-bit capable processor) but they weren't having it. Naturally, I decided to do it anyway. I downloaded a copy of Windows 10 from the Microsoft website and used Rufus to make a bootable USB. I then installed Windows 10 64-bit on the computer, and was finally able to use all 4GB of RAM. After installing countless optimization mods, I was finally able to play Minecraft in 60fps in an 800x600 window.

In seventh grade, I finally got a new computer - a Chromebook. 13-year-old me was less than thrilled about it, and within just a few months I figured out how to install Linux on it. I purchased a 64GB USB drive and installed Arch Linux on it, and was finally able to use a proper computer - well, as proper as a Chromebook can get. I also figured out how to dual-boot on my old computer, and installed Ubuntu 20.04 on it.

## New Computer

In eighth grade, I finally got an actual computer for my birthday - an Asus TUF F15 something something. With an i7-11370H, 16GB of RAM, and an RTX 3050 Ti, it was a huge upgrade from my Optiplex 780 and Chromebook. I was finally able to play games at 1080p 144fps, and could also compile code in reasonable amounts of time. I also got really into crypto mining, and was able to make almost a hundred dollars mining various altcoins on that laptop (the 3050 Ti didn't have enough VRAM to load Ethereum's DAG lol).

Unfortunately, I was also a bit of a klutz and dropped my "TUF" laptop nearly 20 times over the span of just two years. By the time I started tenth grade, it was falling apart. In January that year, after my laptop basically stopped working entirely, I decided to build a desktop computer. I purchased an AMD Ryzen 7 7700X, 32GB of RAM, and an RTX 4070 Super, and built a computer that was more powerful than anything I had ever used before. Meanwhile, my gaming laptop still to this day sits in my bedroom, acting as a headless server. The screen is completely broken (with the exception of a few rows of pixels functioning on the top) and the GPU doesn't work anymore, but it still works as an excellent server for my various projects (e.g. PZChessBot's lichess bot runs on it!).

Occasionally I need to fix something on my laptop, and it's almost like a fun puzzle or escape room to figure out how to fix something that can barely communicate with you. Last time there was a power outage, the local IP of my laptop got reassigned and I couldn't figure out how to SSH into it. By using keyboard shortcuts, I was able to open a fullscreen terminal and with some commands set the title bar of the terminal to the IP address of the laptop (hence displaying it on the few working pixels of the screen). I then used that IP to SSH into the laptop and fix the issue.

## Conclusion

Occasionally, I still find myself fondly looking back at the days where I was constantly in a battle against both my parents and my old computer to get what I wanted. I don't miss the roaring fans, the 5-minute boot times, or the graphics drivers that broke every week. But I do miss the feeling of accomplishment when I finally got something to work, and the satisfaction of figuring out how to do something either my computer wasn't supposed to be able to do or my parents didn't want me to do.

Modern technology is becoming more accessible and user-friendly, but at the same time, overall technical literacy is declining to the point where many people don't even understand where files get downloaded to. The systems we struggle against are often the ones that teach us the most, and I worry perfect technology will leave fewer reasons to look under the hood.
